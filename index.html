White Hat Write Up

Our question is how often allegations against NYPD officers are substantiated, and whether that depends on the type of allegation or the officer's rank.

The data comes from ProPublica, which got the records from New York City's Civilian Complaint Review Board (CCRB) after the state repealed the law that kept police discipline records secret. It has 33,358 allegations from 12,056 complaints against 3,996 officers, for cases closed between September 1985 and January 2020. Each allegation has one of three outcomes. Substantiated means the CCRB found the officer broke NYPD rules. Exonerated means the incident happened but NYPD rules allowed it. Unsubstantiated means the CCRB could not confirm what happened.

The data is not a random sample. ProPublica only got records for officers still on the force in June 2020 who had at least one substantiated allegation, and it left out allegations the CCRB ruled unfounded. Both of these make the substantiation rate in this data higher than for all complaints the CCRB gets, so we say this right under the charts.

Data transformations: the raw data has nine kinds of substantiated outcome, such as Charges or Command Discipline, which mostly describe the discipline the board recommended. We grouped them into one Substantiated group, since our question is whether the allegation was confirmed, not the punishment. We kept Exonerated and Unsubstantiated separate because they mean very different things. We used rank at the time of the incident instead of the officer's current rank, and grouped Captain and all higher ranks together because each of those ranks has very few allegations. Then we counted allegations for every type, rank and outcome, and turned the counts into shares of each bar.

Design choices: we used 100% stacked horizontal bars because the question is about parts of a whole. Out of all allegations of one kind, what share ended each way? Every bar uses the same scale from 0% to 100%, so the bars can be compared directly. Allegation types are sorted by substantiation rate and ranks are in rank order. Substantiated is the darkest color and starts at the left edge, so it is easy to compare across bars. We avoided red and green so the colors don't suggest an outcome is good or bad. Each bar shows its total number of allegations so readers can see that some groups are small, and hovering over a bar shows the exact count. The labels say allegations, not complaints, because one complaint can include several allegations.

Other options we considered: a pie chart for each type was harder to compare, and bars of raw counts made Abuse of Authority look like it had the most of every outcome just because it has the most allegations.
