NYPD Complaint Outcomes: White Hat and Black Hat

Our question: how often are complaints against NYPD officers substantiated, and does it depend on the type of allegation or the officer's rank? Below are two charts made from the same data. The white hat chart tries to answer the question honestly. The black hat chart is made to mislead on purpose.

White Hat Write Up

Our question is how often allegations against NYPD officers are substantiated, and whether that depends on the type of allegation or the officer's rank.

The data comes from ProPublica, which got the records from New York City's Civilian Complaint Review Board (CCRB) after the state repealed the law that kept police discipline records secret. It has 33,358 allegations from 12,056 complaints against 3,996 officers, for cases closed between September 1985 and January 2020. Each allegation has one of three outcomes. Substantiated means the CCRB found the officer broke NYPD rules. Exonerated means the incident happened but NYPD rules allowed it. Unsubstantiated means the CCRB could not confirm what happened.

The data is not a random sample. ProPublica only got records for officers still on the force in June 2020 who had at least one substantiated allegation, and it left out allegations the CCRB ruled unfounded. Both of these make the substantiation rate in this data higher than for all complaints the CCRB gets, so we say this right under the charts.

Data transformations: the raw data has nine kinds of substantiated outcome, such as Charges or Command Discipline, which mostly describe the discipline the board recommended. We grouped them into one Substantiated group, since our question is whether the allegation was confirmed, not the punishment. We kept Exonerated and Unsubstantiated separate because they mean very different things. We used rank at the time of the incident instead of the officer's current rank, and grouped Captain and all higher ranks together because each of those ranks has very few allegations. Then we counted allegations for every type, rank and outcome, and turned the counts into shares of each bar.

Design choices: we used 100% stacked horizontal bars because the question is about parts of a whole. Out of all allegations of one kind, what share ended each way? Every bar uses the same scale from 0% to 100%, so the bars can be compared directly. Allegation types are sorted by substantiation rate and ranks are in rank order. Substantiated is the darkest color and starts at the left edge, so it is easy to compare across bars. We avoided red and green so the colors don't suggest an outcome is good or bad. Each bar shows its total number of allegations so readers can see that some groups are small, and hovering over a bar shows the exact count. The labels say allegations, not complaints, because one complaint can include several allegations.

Other options we considered: a pie chart for each type was harder to compare, and bars of raw counts made Abuse of Authority look like it had the most of every outcome just because it has the most allegations.

Black Hat

Black Hat Write Up

Our black hat chart is designed to make readers believe that NYPD officers are almost always cleared. It uses the same cleaned CSV as the white hat charts and combines all officer ranks within each allegation type. The misleading story comes from how we group, describe and emphasize the outcomes, rather than from changing the original counts.

First, we merge Exonerated and Unsubstantiated into one category. Exonerated means the incident happened but NYPD rules allowed it. Unsubstantiated means the board could not establish what happened. Combining them hides an important difference between permitted conduct and insufficient evidence.

Second, we label the combined category Officer cleared. This suggests a stronger conclusion than the data supports because an unsubstantiated allegation does not establish that the officer acted properly. The title reinforces this interpretation. About 75% of allegations fall into the combined category, but describing that as almost always cleared exaggerates both the frequency and the meaning of the result.

Third, the subtitle, axis, totals and tooltips call allegations complaints. The dataset contains 33,358 allegations from 12,056 complaints. One complaint can include several allegations, so the wording makes the number of separate complaints appear much larger. The percentages therefore describe allegations, even though the chart presents them as complaint outcomes.

Fourth, dark blue makes the combined category visually dominant. Substantiated outcomes use pale gray that blends into the white background. Only the combined category receives percentage labels inside the bars. Segment widths remain proportional, but the contrast and selective labels encourage readers to overlook confirmed misconduct.

A full 0% to 100% axis, a legend, exact tooltip values, a note that defines each outcome and a source line make the chart appear careful and trustworthy. The note says an officer was cleared when the board did not substantiate the complaint. That sentence is true, but next to the label Officer cleared it reads as if the officer was found innocent. The source line omits the dataset's selection bias. These familiar presentation choices help the misleading interpretation seem credible despite the problems explained above.

Development Process

We picked the dataset, the question, the black hat story and the four tricks together before anyone started, and wrote the plan in PROJECT.md. That way each person could work on their own time without waiting on the others. Everyone logged their hours and AI use in LOG.md, which is where the numbers below come from.

Who did what. Person A (Mouhamadou) downloaded and cleaned the data, set up the repo and the page, built the two white hat charts, and wrote the white hat write up and the references. Person B built the black hat chart and wrote the black hat write up. Person C (Buruk) checked both charts against each other and polished them, wrote this section and the AI usage section, ran the AI experiment described below, recorded the demo video and made the submission zip.

People hours. Person A spent about 1 hour, Person B about 2 hours and Person C about 2 hours, so the project took about 5 people hours in total.

What took the longest. The black hat chart and its write up took the most time. The hard part was keeping every trick subtle. The chart had to mislead while still having everything a trustworthy chart has, so we ruled out a truncated axis, 3D and pie charts and relied on grouping, wording and color instead. Data cleaning took less time than we expected, because the outcome and rank columns only needed to be grouped. For Person C, most of the time went into the AI experiment, since we had to crop each chart so the AI could not see the words White Hat or Black Hat, and then compare its answers against our list of tricks.

Polish changes Person C made. We gave both charts the same axis ticks (every 10%), so neither looks less careful than the other. The white hat chart already had a note defining each outcome, so the black hat got one too, because a chart without definitions looks less trustworthy. The black hat already had a source line, so the white hat got one too. Both notes now tell readers they can hover over a bar for exact numbers. We also tested the page in the browser and checked the console for errors.

AI Experiment: Can an AI Spot Our Black Hat Tricks?

We wanted to use AI as a test reader, not only as a code writer. Our black hat chart is supposed to fool a careful person, so we asked whether it would fool an AI that reads charts for a living. We took a screenshot of each chart and cropped it so the words White Hat and Black Hat did not show. Then we showed each image to fresh Claude instances that had no access to our files, our plan or this conversation. Each chart got two prompts. The naive prompt was "A friend sent me this chart. What does it tell me?" The skeptical prompt was "Is this chart fair and accurate? Point out anything that could mislead a reader." That gave four runs in total. The full answers are saved in ai_experiment/ in our repo.

TrickNaive promptSkeptical prompt
1. Exonerated and Unsubstantiated mergedPartly. It said cleared lumps several outcomes togetherCaught
2. Merged group called Officer clearedPartly. It said cleared does not mean innocent, but repeated the headline anywayCaught, high confidence
3. Allegations called complaintsFooled. It said "33,358 civilian complaints"Caught, but only from memory of the dataset
4. Dark cleared bars, pale substantiated barsFooled. Never mentionedCaught, medium confidence
Hidden dataset biasNot mentionedCaught, medium confidence

What happened. With the naive prompt, the AI believed the story. Its main takeaway was that about 3 in 4 of 33,358 civilian complaints ended without a finding against the officer, and it pushed the story further than our title did by pointing out that Force, the most serious type, had the highest cleared rate. It did add a caution that cleared only means not substantiated, but that came from the definitions note we added during polishing. Its caution also included a mistake. It said cleared could mean the officer was shown not to have done it, but those unfounded allegations are not in this data at all. With the skeptical prompt, the same model caught all four tricks and the hidden bias. It checked that the totals add up, said the layout was honest, and blamed the wording. For the white hat chart, both prompts gave an accurate summary, and the skeptical run found no visual tricks.

What we learned. First, the question mattered more than the chart. The same model was fooled when asked casually and caught everything when asked to look for problems, and most real readers look at charts casually. Second, the AI trusted words more than pictures. Even when skeptical, it rated our color trick only medium confidence and called it subjective, while it was sure about every label problem. Our quietest trick was also our hardest to catch. Third, the complaints trick could not be caught from the chart at all. The skeptical run only caught it because it remembered that the ProPublica data has one row per allegation and about 12,000 complaints. A reader who does not already know the dataset has no way to notice, which is why we think it is our strongest trick. Fourth, adding a note that looks careful made the chart more trustworthy but also gave readers a clue, since the note says did not substantiate right next to the label Officer cleared. A real bad actor would probably leave that note out.

The AI was also useful as a reviewer for our white hat. The skeptical run pointed out that the warning about who is in the data sat in small print under the charts, far from the 25% headline, and that the chart never said which years it covers. We fixed both: the headline now says the rate is for this data and points to the note, and the note gives the years.

Limits. We ran each prompt once, so this is a small test, not a study. All four runs used Claude, the same family of model that helped build the page, so another chatbot might do better or worse. The test instances could not see our files, but the folder name of the screenshots included the project name, which could have hinted that a chart might be misleading.
